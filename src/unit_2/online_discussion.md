Hi Abinel,

Your discussion provides a strong explanation of how fully digital enterprises rely heavily on interconnected systems and cloud-based technologies. I particularly agree with your point regarding the increased attack surface created by digital transformation. As organisations become more dependent on networked systems, vulnerabilities within a single component can potentially compromise the entire infrastructure.

One additional point worth considering is the importance of implementing a “zero trust” security model within digital enterprises. According to Kindervag (2010), zero trust architecture assumes that no user or device should automatically be trusted, even within the internal network. This approach can reduce the risk of insider threats and unauthorised access, especially in organisations using remote working and cloud services. Furthermore, many cyber incidents occur because organisations fail to apply regular security patches and software updates. Effective patch management and vulnerability assessments could therefore help SMEs reduce exposure to ransomware attacks such as WannaCry (National Cyber Security Centre, 2018).

I also agree with your argument that SMEs often lack cybersecurity expertise and staff awareness. In many cases, employees become the weakest link in security because phishing and social engineering attacks target human behaviour rather than technical systems. Security awareness training and simulated phishing exercises can therefore play a critical role in reducing successful attacks (Whitman and Mattord, 2021).

Overall, your post effectively highlights the relationship between digital transformation and cybersecurity risk. However, integrating proactive security frameworks, continuous monitoring, and employee-focused security culture could further strengthen organisational resilience against evolving cyber threats.

Hi Victor,

Your post provides a good overview of the relationship between digital transformation and cybersecurity, particularly regarding vulnerability management and legal compliance. I agree with your argument that organisations must prioritise vulnerabilities through risk assessment because cyber threats are continuously evolving and can significantly disrupt business operations if not managed effectively.

One important issue that could further strengthen your discussion is the role of human factors in cybersecurity. Although technical controls such as automation and patch management are essential, many cyberattacks succeed because employees are unaware of phishing techniques or poor security practices. According to Whitman and Mattord (2021), employee awareness training is a critical component of information security because human error remains one of the leading causes of data breaches. Therefore, organisations should combine technical controls with continuous staff education and security awareness programmes.

I also agree with your point about the risks associated with system misconfiguration during digital transformation. Misconfigured cloud environments and poorly secured network settings are common causes of security incidents in organisations adopting new technologies rapidly. Implementing security-by-design principles and conducting regular security audits can help reduce these risks (Anderson, 2020). In addition, SMEs transitioning to digital operations should consider adopting multi-factor authentication, encryption, and backup recovery strategies to improve resilience against ransomware and unauthorised access.

Overall, your post effectively highlights the importance of balancing innovation, operational efficiency, and cybersecurity governance within modern digital enterprises.
