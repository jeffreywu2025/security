My post:

A fully digital enterprise is an organisation that relies primarily on digital technologies to deliver products, services, communication, and internal operations. Such enterprises use cloud computing, online platforms, artificial intelligence (AI), big data analytics, and interconnected systems to support business activities and customer engagement. Digital transformation has become a major driver of economic growth and competitiveness because organisations increasingly depend on digital infrastructure and ICT capabilities to operate effectively (Doroiman and Sîrghi, 2024). Examples of fully digital enterprises include online banks, e-commerce companies, and software-as-a-service (SaaS) providers. According to Vial (2019), digital transformation significantly changes organisational structures, operational processes, and customer relationships through the integration of digital technologies into business strategy.

Despite the operational advantages of digital transformation, fully digital enterprises face increasingly sophisticated cybersecurity threats. The dependency on cloud infrastructure, remote access technologies, and interconnected devices significantly expands the organisational attack surface (Schiliro, 2023). As a result, businesses become more vulnerable to ransomware attacks, phishing campaigns, insider threats, malware, and data breaches. These threats can compromise the confidentiality, integrity, and availability (CIA triad) of information systems, which are fundamental principles of information security (Whitman and Mattord, 2021). Furthermore, organisations that fail to protect sensitive customer and organisational data may face financial losses, reputational damage, and legal penalties under regulations such as the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018. Human error also remains a major vulnerability because employees may unknowingly disclose information through weak passwords or social engineering attacks.

For bricks-and-mortar SMEs wanting to become digital enterprises, cybersecurity challenges can be even greater. SMEs often lack financial resources, cybersecurity expertise, and secure IT infrastructure. Many small businesses adopt digital technologies rapidly without implementing appropriate security controls, making them attractive targets for cybercriminals (Doroiman and Sîrghi, 2024). The WannaCry ransomware attack in 2017 demonstrated how organisations with outdated systems and weak patch management practices can suffer severe operational disruption (National Cyber Security Centre, 2018). In addition, SMEs may struggle to comply with legal and ethical responsibilities relating to data protection and privacy due to limited awareness of cybersecurity governance. To reduce these risks, SMEs should implement employee awareness training, secure network architecture, multi-factor authentication, regular software updates, encryption, and continuous risk assessment strategies (Whitman and Mattord, 2021).

In conclusion, although digital transformation creates opportunities for innovation, efficiency, and competitiveness, it also introduces significant cybersecurity risks. Therefore, organisations must integrate cybersecurity into all aspects of business operations to ensure resilience against evolving threats while maintaining customer trust and legal compliance.
